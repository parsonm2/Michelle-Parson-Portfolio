# About Me

## A Little Context

I'm a UX researcher and designer who believes that people are experts in their own lives. The people who use the products and services we design for should be at the center of the design process. The best products are designed with discernment for the interdependent nature of the product's existence within the ecological context of the user's life. As designers, we have an obligation to shape our product for the people using it. This begins with a genuine curiosity about people, their behaviors, goals, motivations, ideas, attitudes, and the friction they encounter and navigate in every day life.

My work sits at the intersection of qualitative research and systematic design. I'm drawn to complex problems that sit in between interdependent societal systems, and how to design these systems with humans who use and live within them in mind to better serve underrepresented communities.

Previously a Coach and Group Fitness Class Leader, my experience personalizing experiences for people has equipped me to design with a human-centered approach. Currently open to new collaborations.

Connecting people through proximity and shared interests

Inspired by my research on the relationship between mobility and access to community networks, I wanted to design a cost-effective solution to address the issue of a lack of access to community networks in areas which experience limited mobility. This prototype of a User Interface demonstrates the pillars of community, trust and empathy as integral to our reworked version of a social platform. ThirdSpace is a public service that connects people with similar interests based on proximity, enabling natural friendships to develop through shared location-based experiences.

Inspiration
Our main conversation before starting active development was our primary source of inspiration, along with the talk by Emily Lin and Michelle's extensive prior research on mobility and access to community storytelling networks. The part about breaking down social barriers with a common goal really resonated with us — it became our philosophical anchor throughout the project.

Problem Statement
Modern life has made it increasingly difficult to form organic friendships outside of pre-existing social structures. Dating apps facilitate romantic connection; LinkedIn facilitates professional connection, but no platform addresses the quiet loneliness of living near potential friends you never encounter. Access to community is further constrained by mobility, if you cannot easily get around your neighborhood, your storytelling networks shrink. ThirdSpace sets out to close that gap by leveraging proximity and shared interest to lower the barrier to connection.

How We Built It
Our process began with a roughly three-hour conversation. While we had a LOT of ideas, it was lacking a specific direction, until Emily Lin's talk. Once we had direction, we spent the next few hours at the whiteboards. Our first planning phase captured the essence and philosophy of what we were building, a thesis to act as a lighthouse throughout development. Sitemaps and information architecture followed. Projects without passion or direction can fizzle out, but prior research allowed us to group elements, create connections between pieces of information, and develop coexisting systems. Finally, using our sitemaps, we sketched low-fidelity wireframes. Some screens, particularly the discover panel, took a few iterations. After pre-production, we moved to Figma for core design decisions: font, colors, and component language. We added a checklist to mark what needed to be created and what we could leave out.

User Needs & Objectives
Users need a low-pressure way to meet people who share their interests and live nearby. They want group contexts rather than one-on-one cold introductions, and they want to meet in public, familiar spaces. Trust and safety are paramount, especially for users who may already feel isolated. Our objectives were to design a platform that felt warm and community-oriented rather than transactional, with real places and real accountability built in.

Challenges
Coming from different majors (XA and Information Science), there was a learning curve getting to know each other and how we could best use our strengths. Scope creep is real, and the biggest risk to time-based design challenges like this. Meeting people from the internet has a bad rep for a reason: developing the community trust score and letting the platform moderate itself was a difficult feature to plan for. Adding safety features like the filter and profile badges helped alleviate some potential concerns.

Accomplishments
The map toggle view was a last-minute addition originally thought to be out of scope, while it's a little visually buggy, the effect really sells the gamification aspect and shows the user the groups and friends they could make.

What We Learned
Pre-production and proper planning is very important, however you can get caught in an endless loop of adding features, leading to a cramped and overscoped project. For every 3 ideas you put down in pre-production, removing or merging 2 of them can be really beneficial. This project would not have happened if Ishan and I hadn't been passionate about this subject, this is what can happen when people with similar interests share a goal, much like the app itself. For all the Figma users: use components. They are extremely helpful at making prototypes and removing clutter. Test your prototype on the target device (mobile). Our biggest remaining issue is that some text is quite small, this could have been solved by testing screens on their intended platform.

What's Next for ThirdSpace
A more polished, portfolio version will be created and shared in the coming weeks. Perhaps even a functional version of the app down the road would be a unique project.

## Physical Mobility and Access to Community Storytelling Networks

Examining how mobility infrastructure shapes civic engagement by introducing physical mobility as a structural condition within Communication Infrastructure Theory. How might the way you get to work, school, the library or grocery store enable and/or limit opportunities for social interaction within your community?

View case study →

Implications for Civic Engagement Opportunities

While online platforms expand civic engagement channels, offline participation remains critical to democracy. This study examines how mobility infrastructure shapes citizens' access to local communication environments, which in turn conditions civic and political engagement. Drawing on Communication Infrastructure Theory (CIT), we conceptualize physical mobility as a spatial context influencing one's integration within the neighborhood information ecology.

Purpose & Significance
Transportation infrastructure is rarely discussed as a communication issue, yet how easily a person can move through their neighborhood directly shapes who they encounter, what local institutions they access, and how embedded they become in community life. This study foregrounds mobility as a structural condition of civic communication, with implications for equity in democratic participation.

Theoretical Framework
Communication Infrastructure Theory (CIT; Kim & Ball-Rokeach, 2006) suggests that neighborhood storytelling networks, composed of interpersonal communication, local media, and community organizations, provide informational resources and social ties that facilitate citizen engagement. This study extends CIT by introducing physical mobility as a key Communication Action Context (CAC) factor, operationalized across four dimensions: transit availability and quality, pedestrian infrastructure quality, and neighborhood connectivity.

Research Questions & Hypotheses
H1: ICSN will positively predict (a) place attachment and (b) online civic engagement. H2: Digital media use will positively moderate the relationship between ICSN and (a) place attachment and (b) civic engagement. H3: Higher physical mobility will be positively associated with ICSN. RQ1: Does the relationship between digital media use and civic outcomes vary across different physical mobility conditions?

Methodology
This study adopts a cross-sectional survey design administered via Qualtrics. Participants are undergraduate students from a Midwestern university, asked to report on their high school residential communities. Respondents are first prompted to recall daily travel routines, commonly visited places, and neighborhood accessibility before answering substantive items. Analysis uses moderated mediation (Hayes, 2022 PROCESS macro).

Expected Contribution
This framework highlights the infrastructural conditions of political communication, showing how transit accessibility, walkability, and neighborhood connectivity may shape unequal access to local public-affairs communication. It also resonates with earlier political theory on neighborhood "exit" and "voice," offering a lens to understand fragmented storytelling networks beyond explanations centered on ethnic heterogeneity alone.

Status
Work-in-progress submitted to the AEJMC 2026 Conference Paper Competition under the Research-in-Progress category. Data collection underway (March–April 2026), with analysis and full manuscript preparation planned through summer 2026.

## Best Dressed
A create-your-own-adventure dress-up game demonstrating logic tree structures in Python. Players style an outfit, choose their hair, shoes and accessories for a randomly assigned theme (wedding, night out, or picnic) and receive an ending based on their score.

View case study →
# Tags
Python
UX Design
Interactive Prototyping
2024

Purpose & Context
Built for a Programming class to demonstrate understanding of logic tree structures in Python. The project translates a branching decision system, where each sequential choice narrows the path and accumulates a score, into a playful, visual experience using Pygame. Designing the scoring logic required thinking carefully about user decision flows, a skill directly transferable to UX work.

Logic Tree Structure
The game's core is a branching logic tree. Each player choice (gender, skin tone, hair, outfit, shoes, accessories) is a node with weighted outcomes. Choices are evaluated against the randomly assigned theme (wedding, night out, picnic) and a score is accumulated. The full logic tree diagram and a zoomed detail of one branch are shown below.

How It Works
Players begin at a start screen with background music. After clicking Play, they choose a skin tone and confirm. A random theme is assigned. The player makes four sequential selections,  hair, shoes, outfit, and accessories, each confirmed with a checkmark. Each choice carries a point value relative to how well it matches the assigned theme. A short intermission screen appears before the final outcome is revealed.

Endings & Scoring
The cumulative score across all four choices determines which of three endings the player receives: Good, Neutral, or Bad. Each ending has a themed screen with corresponding music, creating a complete narrative loop. Players can click Play Again to restart with a new random theme.
